How this works, and what we found

What this predicts

This model predicts whether a dengue case will get reported to IDSP (India's Integrated Disease Surveillance Programme) in the coming week(s) — not whether an outbreak actually occurs. That distinction exists because the underlying surveillance data only records what got reported, not ground-truth case counts: reporting itself is gappy and inconsistent (see Limitations below), so "will this get reported" is the honest question the data can answer, while "is an outbreak happening" is not.

Why only Maharashtra / Dengue

This was one of three state-disease combinations tested end-to-end (the other two: Karnataka / Acute Diarrhoeal Disease and Maharashtra / Acute Diarrhoeal Disease). Maharashtra/Dengue is the only one that reliably clears the base rate with statistical confidence — see the full comparison for all three.

Limitations

In plain language: reporting is sparse everywhere, it collapsed hard around 2020, two of the three tested combinations don't reliably beat chance, and the one that does (Maharashtra/Dengue) likely works because it's tracking a slow seasonal pattern rather than real outbreak dynamics. Quoted directly from results/findings.md:

F4 — No state-disease combination in the modeled data has a report in even 40% of the 731 possible ISO weeks (2009-2022); coverage ranges from 13.3% to 36.9%.

Reporting coverage per state-disease is low everywhere, and varies roughly 3x across combos.

F5 — Every one of the six state-disease combinations loses at least 42% of its 2019 reporting rate by 2020, with three combos losing more than 70%.

A near-universal reporting collapse occurs between 2019 and 2020 — most plausibly COVID-19-era disruption to routine surveillance infrastructure (interpretation, not a measured cause).

F6 — Karnataka/Dengue, West Bengal/Acute Diarrhoeal Disease, and West Bengal/Dengue were dropped from the modeling scope; West Bengal/Dengue in particular has as few as 3-4 positive weeks in some splits.

Three combos were excluded from modeling for insufficient positive density.

F11 — As horizon increases from 1 to 4 weeks, Maharashtra/Dengue's test PR-AUC (logistic regression) stays within a narrow band, while Karnataka/ADD's test PR-AUC (XGBoost) decreases at every step.

Interpretation: Dengue's reporting pattern is likely dominated by a slow-moving seasonal regime (so knowing the current reporting-history/seasonal state still carries information 4 weeks out), while Acute Diarrhoeal Disease reporting is driven more by short, localized outbreak dynamics whose signal decays faster than 4 weeks — a proposed explanation, not something measured directly.
